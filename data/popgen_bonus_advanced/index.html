    Part 6 (Bonus) - Population Genomics on the Command Line

    1. Introduction

    In Part 5 you analysed the fire ant SNP data in R, using adegenet to draw a genotype heat map and PopGenome to calculate FST in sliding windows. You were given every line of code.

    This bonus practical asks you to reach the same biological conclusions without R, and without being given the commands. You will use PLINK 2 - the standard command-line toolkit for population-scale genotype data - and then go beyond Part 5 by measuring linkage disequilibrium, the signature that most directly reveals a non-recombining supergene.

    Your dataset is unchanged:

    Genotype label Phenotype description Number of samples
    B single-queen colony 7
    b multiple-queen colony 7

    Fourteen haploid males, across two scaffolds taken from two different chromosomes.

    Warning

    This is a deliberately hard, self-directed exercise. Each section states a goal, not a recipe. Work out the commands yourself from plink2 --help, plink2 --help <flag-name> and the PLINK 2 documentation. Every section then offers a collapsed Hints box (flag names only) and, below it, a collapsed worked command. Open them in that order, and only after you have genuinely tried.

    Info

    No plots, no R. Every result in this practical is read directly in the terminal with head, awk, sort and column. Fourteen samples is small enough that the raw numbers are the visualisation - which is precisely why we can drop the graphics.

    1.1. Fourteen samples is not many

    Almost every design decision below follows from the sample size, so keep these in mind throughout:

    • Within a group of 7 haploids, an allele frequency can only take the values 0, 1/7, 2/7 ... 1. Across all 14, the step is 1/14. Your resolution is blunt.
    • A 10 kb window may contain only a couple of SNPs. A window average built from 2 SNPs is noise, not signal - you will have to filter these out yourself.
    • Linkage disequilibrium estimated from 14 haplotypes is coarse and biased upwards. Rare variants make this far worse, so they must be removed first.
    • With 7 versus 7 haploid genomes, however, a fixed difference (an allele present in all 7 B and absent from all 7 b) is unambiguous and easy to count. Small samples are bad at estimating frequencies but fine at detecting complete separation.

    3. Challenge 1 - Get the Data Into PLINK 2

    PLINK 2 does not work directly from a VCF for most analyses. It converts to its own binary format - a pfile, consisting of .pgen (genotypes), .pvar (variants) and .psam (samples) - and everything downstream reads that.

    Task

    Convert input/snp.vcf.gz into a pfile called tmp/snp.

    Your first attempt will fail. That is intentional. Read PLINK's error message carefully and solve each problem in turn. You need to work out:

    • Why does PLINK reject our chromosome names? (Look at them: bcftools view -H input/snp.vcf.gz | cut -f1 | uniq)
    • Our VCF has . in the ID column. Several later analyses need unique variant IDs. How do you make PLINK generate them from chromosome and position?
    • How do you restrict the dataset to biallelic sites only?
    Hints - Challenge 1

    The four flags you need are among: --vcf, --allow-extra-chr, --set-all-var-ids, --max-alleles, --make-pgen, --out.

    For --set-all-var-ids, the template characters @ and # stand for chromosome and position. Quote the template so the shell does not touch #.

    Stuck? Worked command - Challenge 1 
    plink2 \
  --vcf input/snp.vcf.gz \
  --allow-extra-chr \
  --set-all-var-ids '@:#' \
  --max-alleles 2 \
  --make-pgen \
  --out tmp/snp

    Task

    Verify the conversion rather than trusting it. Compare PLINK's reported counts against the VCF itself:

    bcftools view -H input/snp.vcf.gz | wc -l   # variants in the VCF

cat tmp/snp.psam

    Question

    • How many samples and variants did PLINK load, and do they match the VCF?
    • If the variant count dropped, which flag caused it and is that acceptable?
    • Look at tmp/snp.psam. What are the sample IDs, and what happened to the family ID (FID) column?
    • You should have 14 samples. The variant count should match the VCF unless there were multiallelic sites.
    • Any drop is caused by --max-alleles 2. It is acceptable here: r² and most FST estimators are only defined for biallelic markers, and multiallelic sites in a 14-sample haploid dataset are almost always alignment artefacts.
    • The IDs are the original VCF sample names, still carrying the .bam suffix (e.g. f1_B.bam). There is no FID column at all - the header starts #IID - because a VCF has no family structure, so PLINK 2 does not invent one. You must use the full ID including .bam in every file you write from now on.

    Question

    Each keep file should have 8 lines. Why 8 and not 7?

    Seven sample IDs plus the #IID header line. PLINK 2 uses that header to work out that the file contains IIDs only rather than FID/IID pairs - if you omit it, PLINK will try to interpret your single column as a family ID and match nothing.

    5. Challenge 3 - Who Resembles Whom? A Genetic Relationship Matrix

    In Part 5 you drew a genotype heat map and a PCA to ask a single question: do the B and b individuals fall into two genetic groups, and does that depend on which scaffold you look at?

    With 14 samples there is a more direct answer that needs no graphics at all. Ask PLINK for the genetic relationship between every pair of samples. The result is a 14 × 14 matrix - small enough to read straight off the screen.

    Each off-diagonal value compares two samples across all SNPs, after scaling each SNP by its allele frequency:

    • positive - the pair share more alleles than expected by chance;
    • around 0 - no more or less similar than two random samples;
    • negative - the pair share fewer alleles than expected.

    The diagonal is each sample compared with itself, so it is close to 1.

    Task

    Compute a genetic relationship matrix (GRM) for all samples and write it as a full square matrix to results/rel_all.

    If PLINK refuses to run, read the error carefully. With only 14 samples it does not trust frequencies it estimates on the fly - but you already made a frequency file in Challenge 2.

    Hints - Challenge 3a

    Look up --make-rel with plink2 --help make-rel. The square modifier controls the output shape.

    --read-freq supplies a pre-computed .afreq file. --mac 1 drops sites with no minor allele, which carry no information about relatedness.

    The command writes two files: the numbers (.rel), and the sample IDs in matrix order (.rel.id).

    Note: if you search online you will find --distance. That is a PLINK 1.9 flag and does not exist in PLINK 2.

    Stuck? Worked command - Challenge 3a 
    plink2 \
  --pfile tmp/snp \
  --allow-extra-chr \
  --read-freq tmp/snp.afreq \
  --mac 1 \
  --make-rel square \
  --out results/rel_all

    Task

    The matrix file has no row labels and far too many decimal places. Glue the sample IDs onto the front of each row, round the values to 2 decimal places, and line up the columns.

    Inspect results/rel_all.rel.id first: does it start with a # header line that you need to skip?

    Question

    • On which scaffold do B–B and b–b values differ consistently from B–b values? Describe the pattern.
    • On the other scaffold, do samples still form groups? If so, are those groups related to colony type?
    • On the supergene scaffold, compare the b–b values with the b diagonal values, then do the same for B. Which group is more genetically uniform? Which Part 5 result does this reproduce?
    • This matrix contains the same information as the Part 5 heat map and PCA. Why would this approach stop working if you had 500 samples instead of 14?
    • scaffold_1. It is a perfect checkerboard: every B–b value is about -1.2, every b–b value is about +1.25, and every B–B value is positive (roughly 0.7 to 2.2). The 14 samples fall into two genetic groups that exactly match the phenotype labels. (Sorting the rows so all B come first would turn the checkerboard into two solid blocks.)
    • Yes, but not by colony type. On scaffold_2, pairs such as f2_B/f2b and f7_B/f7b are almost identical to each other, and six samples of both types (f1b, f3_B, f3b, f4_B, f4b, f6b) form a tight cluster. This is the ordinary relatedness and haplotype sharing you expect on a freely recombining chromosome, and it cuts straight across the B/b labels.
    • On scaffold_1, the b–b values (~1.25) are nearly as high as the b diagonal (~1.27-1.33): every b is almost identical to every other b. The B diagonals (2.2-3.5) are much higher than the B–B values, so B individuals differ from each other. This is the low diversity of the b supergene haplotype that PopGenome measured in Part 5.
    • With 500 samples the matrix has 250,000 entries and cannot be read by eye. That is exactly the problem PCA solves: PLINK's --pca is an eigen-decomposition of this same relationship matrix, compressing it into a handful of coordinates that can be plotted. Our sample size is small enough that we can skip the compression step entirely.

    6. Challenge 4 - Genetic Differentiation (FST)

    FST measures how differentiated two groups are at a locus: 0 means the groups are identical, 1 means they share no alleles at all. In Part 5, PopGenome gave you FST averaged over sliding windows.

    Warning

    PLINK 2 has no sliding-window FST option. It will give you FST per variant; turning that into windows is your job. Do not waste time looking for a --fst-window-size flag - it does not exist.

    6.1. Per-variant FST

    Task

    Calculate FST between B and b for every SNP. You need to:

    • load the population labels from tmp/populations.tsv;
    • tell PLINK which column of that file defines the groups;
    • request the Hudson estimator rather than the default;
    • ask for per-variant output as well as the summary.

    Then find out what PLINK actually wrote - the filenames include the population names, so do not guess:

    ls results/fst*
    Hints - Challenge 4a

    --pheno loads the file, --pheno-name picks the column, and --fst takes the name of that same column plus modifiers. Look up the modifiers with plink2 --help fst: you want method=hudson and report-variants.

    A phenotype column containing non-numeric values such as B/b is loaded as a categorical phenotype, which is what --fst requires.

    Stuck? Worked command - Challenge 4a 
    plink2 \
  --pfile tmp/snp \
  --allow-extra-chr \
  --pheno tmp/populations.tsv \
  --pheno-name POP \

head results/fst.*.fst.var
cat  results/fst.fst.summary

    Question

    Why does the choice of estimator matter more here than it would in a study of 2,000 humans? (Hint: read what plink2 --help fst says about the two methods.)

    The Weir–Cockerham and Hudson estimators differ mainly in how they correct for finite sample size. With 7 haplotypes per group those corrections are large relative to the quantity being estimated, so the two methods can give noticeably different numbers. The Hudson estimator is less sensitive to small and unequal sample sizes, which is why it is preferred here. With thousands of samples the correction is negligible and the choice barely matters.

    6.2. Building your own windows

    Task

    Turn the per-variant file into 10 kb non-overlapping windows, reporting for each window: scaffold, start, end, number of SNPs, and mean FST.

    Two traps to handle:

    • The FST column is not in the same position in every PLINK version. Find it from the header line rather than hard-coding a field number.
    • Monomorphic sites give nan. One nan will poison an entire window average.
    • Finally - and most importantly - discard windows built from only a handful of SNPs before you rank anything.
    Hints - Challenge 4b

    int($pos/10000) gives the window index. Build a key from scaffold plus window index, accumulate sum[key] and n[key], and print in an END block. Test a numeric value with ($x+0==$x), which is false for nan.

    Stuck? Worked command - Challenge 4b 
    awk -F'\t' '
  NR==1 { for (i=1; i<=NF; i++) { if ($i ~ /FST$/) f=i; if ($i=="POS") p=i }; next }
  ($f+0==$f) {
    w = int($p/10000)
    key = $1"\t"(w*10000)"\t"((w+1)*10000)
    sum[key] += $f; n[key]++
  }
  END { for (k in sum) if (n[k] >= 5) printf "%s\t%d\t%.4f\n", k, n[k], sum[k]/n[k] }
' results/fst.*.fst.var \
  | sort -k1,1 -k2,2n \
  > results/fst_windows.tsv

head results/fst_windows.tsv

    Task

    Question

    • Which scaffold holds the high-FST windows?
    • Re-run the windowing step with the n[k] >= 5 filter removed. What happens to the top of the ranked list, and why?
    • B and b do not recombine with one another in one region of the genome. Explain the FST pattern in that light.
    • scaffold_1: every one of the top 10 windows is on it, and its mean window FST is about 0.67, compared with about 0.02 on scaffold_2. The genome-wide summary (about 0.63) is dominated by scaffold_1.
    • Without the filter, the top of the list fills with windows containing one or two SNPs and an FST of exactly 1. A single SNP that happens to be fixed between the groups gives a perfect window average, but it is one observation from 14 haplotypes - it tells you almost nothing. Minimum-SNP filters are not cosmetic; with small samples they are what separates signal from arithmetic.
    • scaffold_1 contains the supergene. Because recombination between the B and b haplotypes is suppressed, mutations arising on one haplotype can never be shuffled onto the other, so differences accumulate and become fixed - driving FST up across the whole region. scaffold_2 recombines normally, so alleles continue to be shared between the groups and FST stays near zero.

    6.3. Counting fixed differences

    FST is a summary statistic. With haploid data and clean groups you can ask something more concrete: at how many SNPs does every single B carry one allele and every single b the other?

    Task

    Calculate allele frequencies separately for each group, then compare them. Use the keep-files from Challenge 2.

    Hints - Challenge 4d

    --keep restricts a run to the listed samples. Loop over B and b, writing tmp/freq_B.afreq and tmp/freq_b.afreq. A fixed difference is a variant where ALT_FREQS is 1 in one group and 0 in the other.

    Stuck? Worked commands - Challenge 4e 
    for pop in B b; do
  plink2 \
    --pfile tmp/snp \
    --allow-extra-chr \

awk '
  FNR==1 { for (i=1; i<=NF; i++) col[$i]=i; id=col["ID"]; fq=col["ALT_FREQS"]; ch=col["#CHROM"]; next }
  NR==FNR { fB[$id]=$fq; chrom[$id]=$ch; next }
  ($id in fB) {
    total[chrom[$id]]++
    if ((fB[$id]+0==1 && $fq+0==0) || (fB[$id]+0==0 && $fq+0==1)) fixed[chrom[$id]]++
  }
  END { for (c in total) printf "%s\tSNPs=%d\tfixed_differences=%d\t(%.1f%%)\n", c, total[c], fixed[c], 100*fixed[c]/total[c] }
' tmp/freq_B.afreq tmp/freq_b.afreq

    Question

    • How many fixed differences are there on each scaffold?
    • A sceptic says: "with only 7 samples per group, some of those apparent fixed differences are luck." Are they right, and does that objection apply equally to both scaffolds?
    • scaffold_1 has about 524 fixed differences - nearly half of its 1,106 SNPs. scaffold_2 has none.
    • The sceptic has a fair point in principle: a variant at 80% frequency in a large population can easily appear in all 7 sampled B individuals by chance. For any single SNP the evidence is weak. But the objection does not explain what you see: chance cannot produce hundreds of fixed differences concentrated in one contiguous region of one scaffold while the other scaffold has none. The clustering, not the individual SNPs, is what makes the result convincing - and it is why the next section, which looks at how those SNPs are linked together, matters so much.

    7. Challenge 5 - Linkage Disequilibrium (Going Beyond Part 5)

    Everything so far tells you that B and b differ on scaffold_1. It does not directly show why. The supergene hypothesis makes a specific, testable prediction about linkage disequilibrium (LD): the non-random association between alleles at different positions, measured as r².

    Under normal recombination, LD decays with physical distance - nearby SNPs are correlated, distant ones are not. Inside a region where recombination is suppressed, the whole block is inherited as a single unit, so r² stays high no matter how far apart the SNPs are.

    Warning

    LD from 14 haplotypes is noisy and biased upwards, and rare alleles make this much worse: a variant seen in a single individual is perfectly correlated with every other singleton in that individual, by construction. You must apply a minor-allele-frequency filter before computing r², or your results are an artefact.

    7.1. Calculating r²

    Task

    For each scaffold separately, calculate r² between pairs of SNPs, having first removed low-frequency variants.

    You will need to work out:

    • which r² flag suits unphased genotype data;
    • how to stop PLINK discarding pairs below its default r² threshold (you want the low values too - they are the whole point of a decay curve);
    • how to stop PLINK limiting pairs by variant count and by distance, so that you get comparisons spanning the full scaffold;
    • what MAF threshold corresponds to "seen in at least 2 of the 14 samples".
    Hints - Challenge 5a

    --r2-unphased is the calculation. The relevant windowing flags are --ld-window, --ld-window-kb and --ld-window-r2; check their defaults with plink2 --help ld-window, because the defaults are designed for human genome-wide data and will silently throw away most of your pairs.

    --maf sets the frequency filter. 2/14 ≈ 0.14.

    Run ls results/ld_* afterwards - work out the output extension rather than assuming it.

    Stuck? Worked command - Challenge 5a 
    for sc in scaffold_1 scaffold_2; do
  plink2 \
    --pfile tmp/snp \
    --allow-extra-chr \
    --chr "$sc" \
    --maf 0.14 \
    --r2-unphased \

    --ld-window-kb 10000 \

    Question

    Before you look at any r² values, predict: in Challenge 4 you found that around half the SNPs on scaffold_1 are fixed differences between B and b. What must r² be between any two of those SNPs, and how should that depend on the distance between them?

    Exactly 1, at any distance. Every fixed-difference SNP splits the 14 samples into the same two groups (the 7 B and the 7 b), so knowing the allele at one tells you the allele at every other with certainty. With around 500 such SNPs, roughly a third of all scaffold_1 pairs should have r² = 1 regardless of distance. Look for this in the next step.

    7.2. LD decay with distance

    Task

    Build an LD decay table: group SNP pairs by the physical distance between them (for example in 10 kb bins), and report for each bin the number of pairs, the mean r², and the proportion of pairs in near-perfect LD (r² ≥ 0.8).

    As in Challenge 4, locate the columns from the header line rather than hard-coding positions, and require a minimum number of pairs per bin.

    Hints - Challenge 5b

    The columns you need are the two positions and the r² value; the r² header ends in R2. Distance is the absolute difference between the positions. The bin index is int(distance/10000). Keep a third counter for pairs with r² ≥ 0.8.

    Stuck? Worked command - Challenge 5b 
    for sc in scaffold_1 scaffold_2; do
  echo "=== $sc ==="
  awk -F'\t' '
    NR==1 { for (i=1;i<=NF;i++) { if ($i ~ /R2$/) r=i; if ($i=="POS_A") a=i; if ($i=="POS_B") b=i }; next }
    ($r+0==$r) {
      d = ($b > $a) ? $b-$a : $a-$b
      k = int(d/10000)
      sum[k] += $r; n[k]++; if ($r >= 0.8) hi[k]++
      if (k > maxk) maxk = k
    }
    END {
      printf "%-14s %8s %10s %12s\n", "distance_kb", "n_pairs", "mean_r2", "prop_r2>=0.8"
      for (k=0; k<=maxk; k++) if (n[k] >= 20)
        printf "%-14s %8d %10.3f %12.3f\n", (k*10)"-"((k+1)*10), n[k], sum[k]/n[k], hi[k]/n[k]
    }
  ' results/ld_${sc}.vcor
done

    Question

    • Does mean r² decay with distance on either scaffold across the ~200 kb you have?
    • Which scaffold has consistently higher r², and higher proportion of near-perfect pairs? Does that change with distance?
    • scaffold_2 recombines normally, yet its r² (~0.3) is far above what unlinked SNPs should show. Look back at its relationship matrix in Challenge 3. What might be inflating LD?
    • Why is LD a more direct test of the supergene hypothesis than FST?
    • Not in any clear way. scaffold_1 sits at about 0.55 and scaffold_2 at about 0.30 across every bin; scaffold_2 drops only slightly from its first bin (about 0.36 at 0-10 kb). A 200 kb scaffold and 14 samples are simply not enough to trace a full decay curve - an honest and important limitation.
    • scaffold_1, at every distance. Roughly a third of its pairs (0.30-0.41) have r² ≥ 0.8 whether they are 5 kb or 195 kb apart, against 0.06-0.15 on scaffold_2. This is your prediction from 7.1 coming true: the fixed-difference SNPs are all in perfect LD with each other, 10 bp or 190 kb apart. Nothing has broken the B and b haplotypes up.
    • Relatedness. The relationship matrix showed near-identical pairs (f2_B/f2b, f7_B/f7b) and a tight six-sample cluster. Related individuals share long stretches of chromosome, which creates LD between distant SNPs even where recombination is normal. With 14 samples, a few such pairs are enough to lift the whole curve. This is also why PLINK warns about estimating LD from fewer than 50 samples.
    • High FST says the groups differ, but drift, isolation or selection can all produce that. The LD result is more specific: across the whole of scaffold_1, the same two haplotypes persist intact over the full length of the scaffold. That is what suppressed recombination - the defining property of a supergene - looks like.
